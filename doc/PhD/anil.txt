
### Main scientific strengths

The project has several strong features:

- It addresses a real bottleneck in nuclear many-body theory: identifying and diagonalizing the physically relevant part of an enormous configuration space.
- SQD is naturally compatible with the shell model, where basis states can be represented as Slater determinants and encoded by bit strings.
- The applicant appears to have an unusually relevant combination of expertise in shell-model calculations, ab initio interactions, Monte Carlo sampling, HPC implementation, and quantum-computing applications.
- The approach is testable because light nuclei and restricted valence spaces allow exact diagonalization.
- Symmetries such as particle number, parity, angular momentum, and possibly isospin provide valuable structure that can reduce sampling noise and improve the classical reconstruction.
- The project could create useful open-source software connecting quantum-circuit sampling to established nuclear-structure codes.

### Central scientific risks

#### 1. SQD does not remove the exponential problem automatically

SQD avoids constructing and diagonalizing the full Hilbert-space Hamiltonian only if a sufficiently small and physically representative subspace can be identified. The method therefore moves the main difficulty to:

- preparing a quantum state with adequate overlap with the relevant eigenstates;
- obtaining enough samples to discover important configurations;
- determining how many configurations are needed;
- and controlling the classical diagonalization cost of the selected subspace.

The proposal should state explicitly that SQD is a subspace-selection
method, not a general solution to the exponential scaling problem. Its
success depends on the effective sparsity or compressibility of the
target wavefunction in the chosen basis.

#### 2. The source of the quantum advantage is not yet clear

The proposal assumes that a quantum circuit will generate better
samples than classical methods, but this is not established. The
comparison should include classical alternatives such as:

- Monte Carlo shell model sampling;
- variational Monte Carlo;
- RBM or neural-network quantum states;
- selected-CI and importance-truncated shell-model methods;
- and adaptive basis-selection schemes.

The key question could be:

> Under what conditions does quantum-generated sampling identify a useful subspace with fewer samples or lower computational cost than the best available classical sampler?

Without this comparison, the project risks becoming an implementation
of SQD rather than a demonstration of a clear scientific advantage.

#### 3. Symmetry restoration needs more careful formulation

Discarding bit strings that violate particle number is
straightforward. Angular momentum is more difficult. A
computational-basis Slater determinant is generally not an eigenstate
of total angular momentum, and simply filtering configurations will
not restore good \(J\). Proper angular-momentum restoration generally
requires projection operators, symmetry-adapted bases, or group
integration.

This should be separated into levels of difficulty:

1. exact particle-number and parity filtering;
2. proton/neutron-number and other discrete symmetries;
3. angular-momentum projection;
4. approximate restoration under noisy sampling.

The proposal should avoid implying that all these symmetries can be
handled by the same post-processing step.

#### 4. “Variational” needs precise use

Diagonalizing the Hamiltonian in a selected subspace gives a
variational upper bound to the exact ground-state energy, provided the
selected basis states are valid states in the target Hilbert space and
the projected Hamiltonian is constructed correctly. However, the
sampled probability distribution itself is not variational, and the
quality of the result depends on whether the subspace contains the
important configurations.

For excited states, the situation is more delicate: one must specify
whether the method uses direct diagonalization, orthogonality
constraints, deflation, quantum subspace expansion, or state-specific
sampling.

#### 5. The near-term hardware claims are probably too optimistic

The project can be scientifically valuable using circuit simulation,
emulators, and small quantum processors. It should not rely on the
assumption that current hardware will reach medium- or heavy-mass
shell-model calculations. The main near-term deliverable should be a
validated algorithmic framework, not a claim of immediate
nuclear-structure capability at large scale.

Statements such as “the predictive power will enhance as the number of
qubits increases” should be replaced by measurable quantities:
subspace overlap, energy error, number of samples, circuit depth,
number of qubits, and classical post-processing cost.

## Evaluation of the proposed work packages

### WP1: SQD for nuclear shell-model Hamiltonians

This is the strongest work package and should be the scientific core.

It should begin with a formal definition of the algorithm:

1. define the nuclear Hamiltonian and qubit encoding;
2. prepare or approximate a trial state;
3. sample configurations from the quantum circuit;
4. construct a selected basis;
5. build the Hamiltonian matrix in that basis;
6. diagonalize it classically;
7. iterate the sampling and basis-selection procedure if needed.

The benchmarks should include exactly solvable pairing and Lipkin
models, followed by small realistic shell-model spaces. For every
benchmark, report:

- ground-state energy error;
- overlap with the exact state;
- excitation energies;
- number of selected configurations;
- number of quantum samples;
- number of Hamiltonian matrix elements;
- circuit depth and two-qubit gate count;
- and classical memory/time.

The proposed two-month interface milestone is feasible. The no-core
shell-model application and medium-mass scalability study should be
treated as later milestones, not early deliverables.

### WP2: Measurement-based and quantum-assisted sampling

This work package is promising but currently too vague. It should be
reframed around the question of how to improve the proposal
distribution from which configurations are sampled.

Possible strategies include:

- symmetry-preserving ansätze;
- adaptive VQE or ADAPT-VQE;
- RBM or neural-network quantum states;
- quantum-assisted variational Monte Carlo;
- importance sampling based on approximate amplitudes;
- adaptive inclusion of configurations;
- and hybrid classical–quantum Markov chains.

The proposal should compare these approaches systematically. It should
not assume that VQE automatically generates useful samples; in many
cases, VQE may itself suffer from barren plateaus, local minima,
symmetry breaking, or insufficient expressivity.

A particularly valuable contribution would be an adaptive SQD loop:

1. sample from the current trial state;
2. construct the selected subspace;
3. diagonalize the subspace Hamiltonian;
4. use the resulting eigenvector to update the trial circuit or classical sampler;
5. repeat until the energy and subspace stabilize.

This would give WP2 a clear algorithmic identity.

### WP3: Extension to the no-core shell model

This is scientifically ambitious but probably too large as a primary
objective for a postdoctoral fellowship unless it is sharply limited.

A realistic version would begin with:

- very light nuclei;
- small no-core spaces;
- exact or Lanczos benchmarks;
- and interactions for which matrix elements can be generated reliably.

The first objective should be to demonstrate that the SQD-selected
subspace reproduces known no-core shell-model results. Extending to
medium-mass ab initio calculations should be presented as a
longer-term direction or optional final milestone.

WP3 should also address the cost of constructing matrix elements in a
no-core basis, the number of qubits required, antisymmetry,
center-of-mass contamination, and the difficulty of obtaining
physically meaningful excited states.

### WP4: Training and dissemination

This is appropriate as an implementation package, but it should not be
counted as a scientific work package. It should include concrete
training goals:

- quantum-circuit design and fermion-to-qubit mappings;
- noise modelling and error mitigation;
- HPC workflow integration;
- software engineering and open-source development;
- and training in project management, supervision, and grant writing.

## Suggested scientific projects

### Project 1: Symmetry-aware SQD benchmark suite

Develop a benchmark suite containing pairing, Lipkin, Hubbard, and
small shell-model Hamiltonians. Compare SQD with exact
diagonalization, MCSM, RBM-VMC, selected-CI, and VQE.

Key outputs:

- energy and overlap errors;
- sample complexity;
- subspace-size scaling;
- robustness to noise;
- and a public benchmark repository.

This would provide the methodological foundation for the whole programme.

### Project 2: Adaptive quantum-selected configuration interaction

Develop an iterative method in which quantum samples select
configurations and classical diagonalization returns improved
amplitudes. The improved state is then used to update the sampling
circuit.

This would combine ideas from SQD, selected-CI, importance truncation,
and adaptive VQE. The central question would be whether the iterative
loop reaches a high-quality subspace with substantially fewer samples
than one-shot sampling.

### Project 3: Symmetry-preserving quantum sampling for nuclei

Construct quantum circuits that preserve particle number, parity,
proton/neutron number, and, where possible, angular momentum. Compare
symmetry-preserving circuits with symmetry-breaking circuits followed
by classical filtering or projection.

Important questions include:

- Does symmetry preservation reduce sample complexity?
- How much accuracy is lost when symmetry is restored only after measurement?
- Can symmetry information be used as an error-mitigation tool?
- What is the cost of angular-momentum projection?

This could become one of the proposal’s most distinctive nuclear-physics contributions.

### Project 4: Quantum-assisted MCSM and RBM sampling

Use quantum circuits not to replace the MCSM or RBM, but to improve
their proposal distributions. For example, quantum-generated samples
could identify important Slater determinants, while classical MCSM
provides the variational optimization.

This hybrid approach is likely more realistic than claiming that
quantum hardware will directly diagonalize large shell-model
Hamiltonians. It also builds naturally on your existing
experience.

### Project 5: Noise-aware SQD and error mitigation

Study how readout errors, depolarizing noise, coherent gate errors,
and symmetry-breaking errors affect the selected subspace. Develop:

- measurement-error mitigation;
- symmetry verification;
- zero-noise extrapolation;
- Bayesian reconstruction of configuration probabilities;
- and uncertainty estimates for the final eigenvalues.

A strong result would be a quantitative relation between sampling
noise and the probability that important configurations are missed.

### Project 6: Excited-state SQD

Develop a state-specific SQD method for low-lying excited
states. Possible approaches include:

- simultaneous diagonalization in a common selected subspace;
- orthogonality-constrained sampling;
- deflation;
- symmetry-sector separation;
- or targeted sampling using approximate transition operators.

This is important for nuclear spectroscopy and would significantly
broaden the scientific impact beyond ground-state energies.

### Project 7: No-core SQD for light nuclei

Implement the method first for \(^{4}\mathrm{He}\),
\(^{6}\mathrm{Li}\), \(^{8}\mathrm{Be}\), or similarly small
nuclei. Use exact no-core shell-model or Lanczos results as
benchmarks.

The focus could be on:

- basis selection;
- qubit encoding;
- center-of-mass treatment;
- realistic interaction matrix elements;
- and scaling with oscillator shells.

This is a credible bridge from the shell model to ab initio nuclear calculations.

### Project 8: Resource theory of SQD

Develop a formal resource analysis separating:

- number of qubits;
- circuit depth;
- number of shots;
- acceptance probability;
- number of selected configurations;
- classical diagonalization cost;
- and total wall-clock time.

This would allow meaningful comparisons with classical and variational methods and prevent the project from relying only on qubit count as a measure of efficiency.

## Recommended restructuring

I would recommend the following three scientific objectives:

### Objective 1: Establish the foundations of SQD for nuclear many-body Hamiltonians

Develop the encoding, sampling, subspace construction, and exact
benchmarking for solvable and light nuclear systems.

### Objective 2: Develop adaptive, symmetry-aware and noise-resilient sampling

Improve the selected subspace through symmetry preservation, adaptive
iteration, quantum-assisted Monte Carlo, and error mitigation.

### Objective 3: Demonstrate scalability toward realistic nuclear calculations

Apply the validated method to realistic shell-model Hamiltonians and
selected no-core systems, with explicit resource estimates and
comparisons against MCSM, RBM-VMC, selected-CI, and VQE.

This structure is more coherent than the current WP1–WP3 formulation
because it makes each stage scientifically dependent on the preceding
one.


What one may suggest is a narrower and more testable scope. The project coould then promise:

1. a validated SQD implementation for nuclear shell-model Hamiltonians;
2. quantitative comparisons with strong classical and quantum baselines;
3. adaptive and symmetry-aware sampling methods;
4. a noise and resource analysis;
5. and a carefully bounded first extension to no-core calculations.

That would give the fellowship a credible core contribution, a
realistic implementation plan, and a clear pathway toward larger
nuclear systems.
