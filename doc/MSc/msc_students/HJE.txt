# External-examiner assessment

## Overall recommendation

The thesis addresses a relevant and timely problem at the intersection of heterogeneous catalysis, transient kinetic experiments and scientific machine learning. Its central idea is to extend Kinetics-Informed Neural Networks from estimating rate constants for a prescribed mechanism to simultaneously inferring kinetic parameters and selecting an active reaction mechanism from a larger “supermechanism”.

The thesis has a clear motivation, a substantial methodological component, and an important negative result: the proposed M-KINN architecture does not reliably recover the correct mechanism or rate constants for the tested system. The candidate is commendably explicit about this failure and identifies several plausible causes, especially the mismatch between the PDE used to generate the data and the ODE used in the KINN, the competing loss terms, and the ill-conditioned switching problem.

My overall assessment is positive but qualified. The work is suitable as a master’s thesis because it demonstrates independent implementation, model development, numerical experimentation and critical interpretation. However, it does not demonstrate that M-KINN has achieved automated mechanistic elucidation. The conclusions should therefore be framed as a systematic feasibility study and failure analysis, rather than as a successful mechanistic-inference method.

Subject to a satisfactory oral defense and verification of the code and numerical results, I would expect the thesis to be a pass. On a Norwegian A–F scale, my provisional assessment would be around C, with the possibility of B if the candidate can convincingly explain the inverse-problem limitations, establish reproducibility and demonstrate a deeper validation than is currently visible in the thesis.

# 1. Main scientific contribution

The thesis makes three main contributions:

1. It reimplements an existing TAP-KINN model in PyTorch.
2. It extends the model with reaction switches intended to select elementary reactions from a supermechanism.
3. It tests alternative switching, initial-condition and site-balance treatments and documents why the resulting model does not recover the known mechanism reliably.

The third contribution is the most scientifically valuable. The results indicate that the proposed inverse problem is considerably more difficult than a standard parameter-estimation problem. In particular, the thesis demonstrates that a model can achieve moderate trajectory agreement while recovering incorrect reaction switches and substantially incorrect rate constants.

This is an important point for the scientific machine-learning community: low physics and data losses do not automatically imply mechanistic identifiability.

The thesis would be stronger if this negative result were made more explicitly the central scientific outcome. At present, the stated aim is to develop a model that can identify mechanisms, while the results show that the model cannot do so reliably under the tested conditions. The thesis should therefore distinguish clearly between:

- what was implemented;
- what was successfully validated;
- what was not achieved;
- and what was learned about the problem.

# 2. Scientific strengths

## 2.1 Good choice of a difficult and relevant problem

Mechanistic inference from transient TAP data is scientifically meaningful. Unlike ordinary curve fitting, it requires distinguishing among competing mechanisms and estimating parameters in a dynamical system with transport, surface reactions and gas-phase species.

The thesis correctly emphasizes that conventional methods usually assume a mechanism in advance. The attempt to use a supermechanism with trainable switches is therefore conceptually interesting.

## 2.2 Good integration of chemistry and machine learning

The candidate has made a serious effort to explain both domains:

- heterogeneous catalysis;
- elementary reaction kinetics;
- microkinetic modelling;
- TAP reactor physics;
- feedforward neural networks;
- PINNs and KINNs;
- differentiable optimization;
- straight-through estimators;
- and constrained loss functions.

This is appropriate for an interdisciplinary master’s thesis.

## 2.3 The candidate is appropriately critical of the results

The thesis does not disguise the weak performance. It reports:

- \(R^2\) values around 0.54–0.56;
- MSE values around 0.008–0.011;
- poor agreement for several gas and surface species;
- incorrect reaction switches;
- and large deviations in predicted rate constants.

The conclusion explicitly states that the method is not yet appropriate for reliable mechanism inference. This honesty is a significant strength.

## 2.4 The thesis identifies plausible failure mechanisms

The final discussion identifies several important problems:

1. The data are generated from a PDE-based model, while the learning model uses an ODE approximation.
2. The loss function contains several competing objectives.
3. Mechanism selection through trainable switches is an unstable optimization problem.
4. The model may reduce the loss by switching off reactions rather than recovering the chemically correct mechanism.

These are plausible and technically relevant interpretations.

## 2.5 The structural constraints are sensible in principle

The attempt to impose:

- site balance;
- initial conditions;
- reaction consistency;
- non-negative rate constants;
- and sparse reaction selection

is well motivated. These are the types of constraints that a mechanistic model should incorporate.

# 3. Major scientific concerns

## 3.1 The model is tested on ideal active-zone data rather than realistic TAP observables

This is probably the most important limitation.

The thesis states that the model uses simulated data containing active-zone concentrations and fluxes. It also acknowledges that a practical TAP experiment generally provides outlet fluxes rather than the full concentration and net-flux fields inside the catalyst zone.

Thus, the current study does not yet test the full inverse problem relevant to experiment. It tests an idealized inverse problem with substantially more information than is normally available.

This does not invalidate the work, but it limits the interpretation:

- The thesis demonstrates difficulty even in an idealized setting.
- It does not yet show that M-KINN can analyse real TAP data.
- It cannot yet support the claim of an automated TAP mechanism-elucidation tool.

The thesis should state this distinction prominently in the abstract, conclusions and discussion.

## 3.2 PDE–ODE model mismatch creates a structural inverse problem

The data are generated from a PDE-based transport–reaction model, while the KINN residual is based on a reduced ODE/thin-zone approximation. This means that the true data-generating process is not contained in the model class used for inference.

Consequently, even an ideal optimizer may be unable to recover the true mechanism and parameters. The model may find the mechanism and parameters that best approximate the PDE-generated trajectories within the restricted ODE family.

This is not merely an optimization failure. It is a model-discrepancy problem.

The thesis correctly identifies this issue, but it should go further by distinguishing:

- optimization error;
- measurement/data error;
- discretization error;
- and model-form error.

A useful additional experiment would be to generate data from the same ODE model used in training. If M-KINN still fails under that matched-model condition, the primary problem is optimization or identifiability. If it succeeds for matched data but fails for PDE-generated data, the PDE–ODE mismatch is demonstrably the dominant cause.

## 3.3 Mechanism switches and rate constants are not identifiable as formulated

The reaction rate is represented schematically as

\[
r_i = s_i k_i \psi_i,
\]

where \(s_i\) is a switch and \(k_i\) is a rate constant.

This formulation creates an immediate identifiability problem: only the product \(s_i k_i\) enters the kinetics. If \(s_i\) is reduced while \(k_i\) is increased, the same reaction rate can be obtained. The model therefore cannot uniquely determine both \(s_i\) and \(k_i\) without additional constraints or a carefully designed parameterization.

This issue is especially serious for the sigmoid switching model, where \(s_i\) is continuous. The threshold \(s_i>0.5\) is an arbitrary classification rule, not a consequence of the kinetic model.

The switch-regularization term \(\sum_i s_i\) can also encourage the model to deactivate reactions while compensating through larger rate constants. This may explain false positives and rate-constant errors.

A better formulation would separate the discrete model-selection problem from continuous parameter estimation, for example by:

- enumerating a small set of candidate mechanisms;
- using Bayesian model comparison;
- using reversible-jump or sparsity-inducing methods;
- optimizing reaction inclusion with a properly normalized parameterization;
- or fixing the scale of active reactions before selecting the mechanism.

The thesis should identify this product non-identifiability explicitly as a central limitation.

## 3.4 The supermechanism is too small to establish automated mechanism discovery

The test system contains nine candidate reactions, with three deliberately artificial or inactive reactions. This is a reasonable proof of concept, but it does not yet demonstrate the intended application to large reaction spaces such as combustion chemistry.

The thesis should distinguish between:

- selecting among nine candidate reactions in a synthetic benchmark;
- and searching a chemically realistic supermechanism containing hundreds or thousands of possible steps.

The computational and statistical difficulty increases dramatically with:

- the number of reactions;
- the number of surface species;
- stiffness;
- correlated parameters;
- unobserved intermediates;
- and incomplete observables.

The current results should therefore be described as a small controlled benchmark, not as evidence of scalability.

## 3.5 The mechanistic-consistency loss is heuristic

The mechanism-consistency term attempts to penalize active reactions requiring species that are not supplied or produced. This is an interesting idea, but the formulation should be justified more carefully.

A reaction network can be chemically valid even when an intermediate is not directly supplied externally, provided it is produced by another active reaction. Conversely, the presence of a reactant in the network does not guarantee that the selected mechanism is dynamically viable.

The current consistency expression appears to encode a coarse reachability condition, not full chemical validity. It does not enforce:

- stoichiometric conservation;
- reaction reversibility consistency;
- thermodynamic consistency;
- detailed balance where appropriate;
- or dynamic accessibility over the observed time interval.

The thesis should state clearly that this is a heuristic regularizer rather than a complete mechanism-validity constraint.

## 3.6 Loss weighting is a major uncontrolled degree of freedom

The model uses a composite loss involving:

- data loss;
- physics loss;
- site-balance loss;
- mechanism-consistency loss;
- switch regularization.

The weights are selected by estimating loss magnitudes and then applying manually chosen relaxation factors. The results are highly sensitive to this choice, and the thesis itself concludes that the procedure is insufficient.

This is a central methodological limitation. The candidate should have included at least a modest sensitivity analysis over the loss weights, or an adaptive weighting strategy, to show whether the failure is robust.

The question is not only whether the loss converges. It is whether different weight choices lead to:

- different mechanisms;
- different rate constants;
- different trajectories;
- or different local minima.

The current results show several local or structurally distinct solutions, but the thesis does not quantify that variability.

## 3.7 Limited statistical validation

The thesis appears to focus on a small number of training runs. For a highly non-convex inverse problem, one run per configuration is insufficient to draw strong conclusions about robustness.

The study should report:

- multiple random seeds;
- mean and standard deviation of trajectory errors;
- mechanism-selection accuracy;
- parameter errors;
- frequency of false positives and false negatives;
- and convergence or failure rates.

A single successful or unsuccessful run cannot establish reliability.

## 3.8 No serious baseline comparison for mechanism inference

The thesis compares variants of M-KINN but does not appear to compare against strong non-neural baselines.

Relevant baselines would include:

- nonlinear least squares with the correct mechanism;
- nonlinear least squares over each candidate mechanism;
- sparse regression such as SINDy-type approaches;
- Bayesian parameter estimation;
- profile likelihood;
- AIC/BIC or likelihood-based mechanism selection;
- regularized kinetic regression;
- and direct integration plus gradient-based optimization.

Without these baselines, it is unclear whether M-KINN offers any advantage over conventional approaches or whether the problem itself is underdetermined.

For an external examiner, this is an important question: is the proposed method failing because the neural architecture is inadequate, or because the available data do not identify the mechanism at all?

## 3.9 The rate-constant estimates are physically and numerically unstable

The tables show predicted rate constants that differ by orders of magnitude from the ground truth, while some trajectories are nevertheless moderately reproduced.

This is a classic symptom of parameter non-identifiability or compensating errors. The thesis should not interpret rate-constant prediction as successful unless it reports:

- relative and absolute errors;
- confidence intervals;
- correlations among fitted parameters;
- sensitivity of the observables to each parameter;
- and whether multiple parameter sets produce essentially identical trajectories.

The conversion from rate constants to activation free energies through the Eyring equation also requires care. A fitted effective rate constant from a reduced model is not automatically a physically meaningful elementary-step rate constant.

# 4. Assessment of methodology

## 4.1 The theoretical background is adequate but sometimes too descriptive

The thesis gives a broad introduction to catalysis, kinetics, TAP, machine learning, PINNs and KINNs. This is appropriate for accessibility.

However, some sections could be more critical and mathematically precise. In particular, the thesis should emphasize:

- the inverse nature of the problem;
- identifiability;
- model discrepancy;
- stiffness;
- parameter correlations;
- and uncertainty quantification.

These are more central to the thesis than a general introduction to feedforward networks.

## 4.2 The model design is conceptually reasonable

The M-KINN architecture is a reasonable first construction:

- neural network outputs concentration and coverage trajectories;
- kinetic parameters are trained;
- reaction switches select reactions;
- physics residuals enforce the governing equations;
- additional terms impose site and mechanistic constraints.

The architecture is understandable and reproducible in principle.

The main issue is that several difficult problems are combined simultaneously:

- trajectory representation;
- inverse parameter estimation;
- reaction selection;
- site-balance enforcement;
- initial-condition treatment;
- PDE–ODE approximation;
- and multiple competing loss terms.

For a first thesis, this is ambitious. A more controlled research design would isolate the difficulties sequentially.

## 4.3 Straight-through estimation requires more careful interpretation

The straight-through estimator allows a hard binary switch in the forward pass while replacing the derivative with an approximate derivative in backpropagation. This is a practical device, not a mathematically exact gradient.

The thesis should discuss:

- the bias introduced by the estimator;
- dependence on threshold and temperature;
- whether the forward and backward objectives are inconsistent;
- and whether switch values are stable across random seeds.

The sigmoid approach avoids the discontinuity but introduces the \(s_i k_i\) identifiability problem and the arbitrary 0.5 threshold.

## 4.4 Site-balance normalization is physically attractive but may affect optimization

Enforcing site balance structurally is generally preferable to imposing it only through a soft loss. However, a normalization transformation changes the geometry of the optimization problem and may create strong correlations among surface outputs.

The thesis correctly observes that the structural normalization did not solve the larger problem. It would be useful to report whether it:

- improved site-balance satisfaction;
- changed gradient magnitudes;
- altered convergence;
- or merely shifted the error among species.

# 5. Assessment of results

The results are valuable primarily because they reveal limitations.

## What the results establish

The experiments establish that:

- the KINN implementation can be re-created in PyTorch;
- the extended model can be trained numerically;
- initial-condition enforcement changes optimization behaviour;
- sigmoid and straight-through switching produce different optimization trajectories;
- structural site-balance enforcement does not solve the mechanism-selection problem;
- and moderate trajectory agreement does not guarantee correct mechanism recovery.

These are valid conclusions.

## What the results do not establish

The results do not establish that:

- M-KINN can reliably infer reaction mechanisms;
- M-KINN can recover rate constants accurately;
- the approach is robust to realistic experimental data;
- the approach scales to large supermechanisms;
- the method is superior to conventional mechanism fitting;
- or the method is ready for practical TAP analysis.

These distinctions should be made explicit.

## Interpretation of the \(R^2\) values

An \(R^2\) value around 0.54 is not a strong fit for a deterministic synthetic-data benchmark, particularly when the model is expected to reproduce governing equations and known concentration profiles. The thesis is right not to describe this as successful.

However, \(R^2\) alone is not an ideal measure for transient kinetics. The thesis should also report:

- relative \(L^2\) trajectory error;
- maximum absolute error;
- errors in peak time and peak amplitude;
- early-time and late-time errors separately;
- errors for gas and surface species;
- and errors in derived observables such as outlet flux.

## The negative result should be more systematic

A strong negative-result thesis would include a diagnostic ladder:

1. matched ODE-generated data, correct mechanism;
2. matched ODE-generated data, mechanism selection;
3. PDE-generated data, correct mechanism;
4. PDE-generated data, mechanism selection;
5. noiseless data;
6. noisy data;
7. ideal active-zone data;
8. outlet-only data.

This would reveal which source of difficulty is dominant.

# 6. Structure, writing and presentation

The thesis is generally well organized:

- introduction;
- theoretical background;
- methodology;
- data;
- results;
- conclusion.

The figures and loss curves help the reader follow the optimization.

There are, however, many editorial issues:

- spelling and grammar errors;
- inconsistent capitalization;
- “referennce”, “swtich”, “adsorped”, “which”/“witch” errors;
- duplicated or awkward phrasing;
- incomplete references or links;
- some captions contain typographical errors;
- equations and symbols occasionally appear inconsistently;
- some conclusions use informal wording such as “the model finds a way”;
- and the thesis sometimes repeats the same interpretation without quantifying it.

The discussion should be edited carefully before final submission. In a computational chemistry thesis, clear definitions of variables, units, model assumptions and data availability are especially important.

The statement that generative AI was used is appropriately included. It should be accompanied by a precise account of what was done with AI-assisted code or text review, especially if code was uploaded or modified.

# 7. Recommended revisions before final submission

## Essential

1. State clearly that the current model is tested only on synthetic active-zone data.
2. Make the PDE–ODE model mismatch central to the interpretation.
3. Discuss the non-identifiability of \(s_i k_i\).
4. Add multiple random seeds or explicitly label the results as single-run diagnostics.
5. Compare against at least one conventional mechanism-fitting baseline.
6. Distinguish trajectory fitting from mechanism identification.
7. Correct the claim that the model “extracts” reliable mechanisms.
8. Report detailed parameter and trajectory errors rather than relying mainly on \(R^2\) and MSE.
9. Explain the mechanistic-consistency loss as heuristic.
10. Verify all tables, equations, references and code links.

## Strongly recommended

11. Add an ablation study for the loss terms.
12. Add a matched-model experiment using ODE-generated data.
13. Test a reduced supermechanism before the nine-reaction system.
14. Report sensitivity to loss weights.
15. Add a conventional sparse or Bayesian mechanism-selection baseline.
16. Discuss stiffness and numerical integration accuracy.
17. Quantify uncertainty in fitted rate constants.
18. Explain the parameterization of non-negative rate constants and its numerical consequences.
19. Clarify what the Eyring-derived activation free energies mean in this reduced model.
20. Add outlet-only data as a clearly separated future or practical scenario.

# Final examiner conclusion

This is a thoughtful and technically ambitious master’s thesis. Its most important achievement is not the successful discovery of reaction mechanisms, but the careful demonstration that a seemingly reasonable KINN extension becomes unreliable when mechanism selection, parameter estimation, physical constraints and model mismatch are combined.

The candidate should receive credit for:

- implementing a nontrivial scientific machine-learning model;
- connecting it to catalytic kinetics and TAP theory;
- carrying out several controlled computational experiments;
- and interpreting negative results critically.

The principal issue is that the thesis initially frames M-KINN as an automated mechanism-elucidation tool, while the results show that it currently functions only as an exploratory prototype under idealized synthetic conditions. A satisfactory defense should show that the candidate understands this distinction, recognizes the identifiability and model-discrepancy issues, and can propose a principled route toward stronger validation.

My provisional recommendation would be: **pass, most likely at C level, with potential for B if the candidate demonstrates strong conceptual command and if the implementation and numerical results are reproducible.**
